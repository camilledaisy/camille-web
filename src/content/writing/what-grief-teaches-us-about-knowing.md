During a night a week before what would have been my father’s fifty-second birthday, I watched the movie, *His Three Daughters*- about three distant sisters who reunite to care for their sick father. In a scene where they gather in the dining hall to discuss his obituary, Christina - the most emotionally restrained of the sisters, reflects on a memory of watching a death scene on TV with their father, who at the time, was grieving the loss of his wife. He becomes visibly upset, insisting that what’s being portrayed isn’t real.

> “The only way to show death is through absence. Everything else is fantasy.”

That line lodged itself in me. Absence, I’ve come to understand, is not simply what remains after someone dies—it is the essence of grief. Not emptiness, not silence, but presence turned inside out. It’s the overwhelming presence of what is no longer here.

There is a paradox in me trying to write about something that resists articulation, in using words to describe what exists beyond language. But I am compelled to try, not because I believe it can solve the mystery of my loss, but because the act of reaching toward understanding, something essential about both grief and love reveals itself.

---

My father died just a few days after my nineteenth birthday, forever linking the celebration of existence with death in ways I’m still learning to separate. His death came without ceremony or closure, no final words of wisdom, no moment of reconciliation, no cinematic farewell. It arrived like fog, slow, quiet, irreversible, transforming the familiar landscape of my life into something distant and unrecognizable.

What shocked me most in the days that followed was how stubbornly normal the world remained, how the sun still rose, people going to work. Life, in its relentless rhythm continues – utterly indifferent to the rupture that split my world in half. That contrast between internal collapse and external continuity became my first lesson in grief’s fundamental dissonance.

Yet, there was this unsettling clarity that accompanies death. Not the kind of enlightenment that’s romanticized, but something rawer and disorienting: the sudden collapse of all the stories we tell ourselves to avoid confronting our collective fate, morality. In his absence, life no longer felt abstract–it felt starkly, terrifyingly real. Death, I learned, is not just the end of a life but the collapse of a my world, the dissolution of an entire framework for meaning-making that had organized my reality around his presence

---

To understand how grief transformed my perception of my father, I must first admit how little I grasped him while he lived. He wasn’t intentionally elusive, but he was impossible to pin down. Where others have learned to smooth their contradictions into something presentable and digestible, he expressed them openly. One day warm and affectionate, the next, withdrawn, angry, and impenetrable. As a child, I found him both magnetic and confusing–he did not offer stability, but mystery.

In his final years, our relationship grew increasingly strained due to unspoken secrets and misunderstandings. The mythology I had constructed around him, of strength, certainty, and invulnerability slowly disintegrated. I could no longer recognize the person I looked up to the most, not after the cracks of the illusions I had of him. It seems that I began grieving him long before he died, mourning not the man himself but the illusion of who I believed him to be.

If I had been so wrong about him–this person I have spent most of my life with–what did that say about the possibility of truly knowing anyone? What did it reveal about the stories we construct about others, the assumptions upon which we build our understanding of the world?

## The Paradox of Posthumous Understanding

Eventually, I began to see him with a clarity that had eluded me in my life. In the stillness he left behind, he came into sharper focus–not as the symbol I had made him, but the deeper recognition that he had been, all along, exactly who he appeared to be: a human being caught between contradictions, struggling with private griefs and unmet longings.

His emotional volatility, once so bewildering, revealed itself as the residue of pain he carried quietly across years. His distance wasn’t rejection but protection.

In *His Three Daughters*, the other two sisters reflect on what their dad meant about grief.

**Katie:** “I think he was trying to say that we won’t really know who he was until after. But I think I know who people are when they’re still living, right?”

**Rachel:** “Yeah, who they are right now… But I think dad meant the whole thing, like all of it you know? All the different times, different people we can be, you know, for it all”

We encounter each other only in fragments, through narrow windows framed by time and circumstance. Only when someone is gone do we begin to glimpse the complete mosaic of their being.

Grief, then, is not merely an emotional response to loss– it’s a way of seeing that becomes available only in the aftermath of absence. Where life had presented my father to me in confusing pieces, death allowed me to perceive the larger pattern, the coherent whole that his contradictions had always composed. It is recognizing that understanding arrives precisely when it can no longer matter to the relationship itself. This is grief’s cruelest paradox: it offers us clarity precisely when understanding can no longer heal anything.

This clarity also forced me to confront a more disturbing truth: I had been using him as a mirror by defining myself in relation to his presence. Without him, I questioned my identity–who am I when stripped of my defining relationships? His death didn’t just take away a person; it dissolved the version of myself that had existed in response to him. I no longer knew who I was supposed to be. The mirror I had used to see myself was gone, the blueprint I relied on was gone. I was left fumbling in the dark, reaching for a reflection that was no longer there.

I had realized how much I had absorbed his patterns of being. His unpredictability had made me a vigilant observer, attuned to every shift in tone and expression. I learned to avoid conflict, conceal discomfort, curating myself into someone agreeable and invisible. When he died, I couldn’t distinguish where his defensive patterns ended and mine began.

In the deepest throes of my grief, where I had learned that escapism and numbing was easier than feeling, I found myself engaging in unconscious imitation. I adopted his mannerisms, his preferences, and even his ways of thinking about the world. At first, this felt like honouring his memory, living out his legacy. But gradually, I realized I was enacting a more desperate strategy: if I could become him, perhaps I could maintain some form of connection.

This mimicry may have felt like a connection, but it was actually a form of disappearance. In trying to preserve him, I was erasing myself. The self that had existed in response to him was trying to survive his death by becoming him entirely. I was not just mourning his absence; I was disappearing into it.

## Analysis

At times, I treated my father as a riddle to be solved. I dissected his life for answers, asked family members about his childhood, traced his behaviours to childhood woods, constructed elaborate theories about his silences. Analysis felt safer than feeling. But in reducing him to a case study, a person to be solved, I lost something essential: the man himself–the flawed, funny, vibrantly ordinary, and annoyingly alive human being in ways that theories couldn’t capture.

This analytical approach was both necessary and insufficient. It was necessary because it allowed me to see him more clearly, to recognize the humanity beneath the mythology I had constructed. But it treated him as an object of knowledge rather than a subject of love, assuming that understanding could substitute for presence, that comprehension could heal the wound of absence.

The living person I had known was irreducible to any analysis–always evolving, always becoming, always escaping the categories I tried to impose. But death had transformed him into something that could be studied, labelled, understood. In gaining the ability to comprehend him, I had lost the mystery that made him himself.

## Living WIthout the Answers

Grief has not delivered resolution. Instead, it has given me questions I am learning to inhabit and live with. The hardest truth i’ve discovered is that we will never fully know another person–not while they breathe, and not after they’re gone. We only know versions of them, filtered through our own needs and projections.

The process of returning to my own existence has been slow and often uncomfortable. I had been someone’s daughter for so long that being simply myself felt like wearing clothes that didn’t fit. But gradually, tentatively, I have begun to recognize which thoughts are mine and which are his echoes. I have started to distinguish between the responses I learned from him and the impulses that arise from my own authentic being.

This is perhaps the existential challenge that loss presents: the call to authentic selfhood that emerges only when our familiar patterns of self-definition are stripped away. Learning to inhabit my own life has meant accepting that some aspects of his influence will always be part of me while still claiming the right to my own separate existence.

## Love Beyond Understanding

His absence has taught me more about love than his presence ever could, not because he failed to love me, but because absence stripped away every illusion, leaving only what truly matters. Love, I believe, is what persists across the fundamental uncertainty of knowing others. We love not despite our inability to fully comprehend each other, but because of it.

Grief has taught me that love requires the preservation of separateness. To truly love someone–living or dead–we must resist the temptation to possess them through understanding, analysis rooted in control, and mimicry. Love, at its deepest, is the capacity to let the other remain other, to honour their irreducible mystery while still maintaining a connection across the gap of unknowing.

In learning to love my father without needing to understand him completely, without needing to become him to stay connected, I have also learned to inhabit my own existence with greater authenticity. His absence, paradoxically, has given me permission to be present to my own life in ways that his overwhelming presence had made difficult.

To love what we cannot possess, it teaches us how to possess ourselves, through the quiet courage to exist authentically in the face of uncertainty. To grieve is to accept that complete knowledge will always elude us. And still, to keep loving. To keep remembering. To keep asking questions, knowing that sometimes the asking itself is enough.
